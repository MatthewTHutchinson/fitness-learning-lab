# How to read fitness research

## The decision-first method

Before reading, write the decision you hope the paper informs. Then extract:

1. **Population:** trained/untrained, age, sex, health, baseline ability.
2. **Intervention:** exact dose, supervision, exercise, duration, adherence.
3. **Comparison:** what did the alternative group actually do?
4. **Outcome:** direct performance, surrogate measure, self-report, or acute
   response?
5. **Magnitude and uncertainty:** not only whether p was below a threshold.
6. **Risk of bias:** randomization, blinding where possible, attrition,
   selective reporting, researcher degrees of freedom.
7. **Directness:** does this answer your situation or require several leaps?

## Evidence hierarchy is not enough

A meta-analysis of small heterogeneous trials can be less useful than a strong,
direct trial for a narrow question. Reviews inherit limitations in their input
studies. Position stands organize evidence but also require inspection of date,
scope, authorship, and conflicts.

## Acute is not chronic

Muscle activation, hormonal responses, soreness, and a single-session power
change do not automatically predict long-term adaptation. Prefer longitudinal
outcomes that match the goal.

## Turn findings into calibrated action

- **Adopt:** evidence is direct, benefit meaningful, cost/risk low.
- **Experiment:** plausible but uncertain; isolate the change and define a stop
  rule.
- **Monitor:** interesting, but no current decision should change.
- **Reject:** poor directness, high cost/risk, or no meaningful advantage.

Use the summary template: citation; question; population; methods; findings;
limitations; practical implication; confidence; what would change the
conclusion.
